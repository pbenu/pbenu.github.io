title: "Cairn"
date: 2026-08-31
standfirst: "A story."

---

Ten men came down under the fig trees. Cornhusk tobacco rolls pulsed in the dark. Now and then a tiny flame leapt and a face briefly emerged behind it. The river sighed on the valley floor. The men sucked their teeth.

At the river, they scoured the caves; they dug through the ash in fishermen's fireplaces for signs of warmth.

"No?"

"Yes. I mean no — no," a man said.

"Boy."

An echo returned from across the river.

"Son."

The father's weak voice had no echo.

They followed the river, calling the boy's name. They searched the trees along the bank and tossed lit resin between the boulders the fishermen hopped across so as not to lose the trail. The monsoon had carried tree stumps down from the mountains and left them along the narrow banks of white sand. The men stopped to turn them over, and to shake the ones lodged beneath the makeshift bridges laid between boulders too far apart to hop.

The river meandered. The men crossed it again and again to stay on the fishermen's trail. At a bend where the crest of a hill narrowed its course, the river gathered into a wide pond. Fishermen swam there in summer. The boy too was a strong swimmer.

One of the men jumped in. He went under, surfaced at the far edge, drew breath, and dived again. When he came out, he shook his head and warmed his palms on a resin torch and lit a stick of tobacco.

"No," he said.

"No," the father said, standing with his back against a boulder.

"It's good that it hasn't rained for a few days," a young man said.

"Child," the father called.

They combed the village's fishing stretch through the night. By the time dawn spilled onto the crests of the mountains, they had built a fire in a cave and were cooking the fish they had caught in the night. They ate rice and fish curry on banana leaves.

In the daylight they split into groups of three and dived in the smaller ponds. In the deeper ones a diver tied a rope around his waist and entered the water. The others, standing outside, held the rope. The diver moved slowly below the surface, feeling with his feet or a stick for hidden shapes. Occasionally the father called out his boy's name.

"No," the men chorused after each dive.

For three hours they dived, surfaced, and said no. By midday the river spread into a wide bottomland. The deepest water reached only to an adult's ankle.

The men returned the way they had come. They cut bamboo poles and sharpened the ends. They dipped the poles into the ponds and felt the beds for shapes. In a small pond they had passed in the night, a pole struck something soft. A man pushed hard on the shape but couldn't pin it down.

"Take the old man away," someone said.

The father turned and walked a little distance up the bank.

More men moved to the boulder by the pond. The man lifted the pole and speared the object with the sharp end. Then several men drove it in. Some pushed one end of the pole down while others pulled it up, levering the shape out of the water. A tan mass rose briefly to the surface and slipped back.

"Can't we hold it firmer?" someone said.

"It's not a small fish down there."

They levered it up again. A sour stench filled the air. They dropped the haul on the sand at the base of the boulder.

Two young men jumped down to inspect it. They covered their mouths with their jackets.

"It's a gazelle."

"No," they chorused to the father, who was still looking away.

More men speared the edges of the pond and pulled out sodden logs. A man speared a fish through its belly. It slapped his wrist with its tail as he wiped away the oozing entrails with litsea leaves before stowing it in a rattan creel at his waist. The fish slapped the creel for some time. The men sucked their teeth. They spoke little except to pass a string of noes down the line along the riverbank.

When they returned to the village in the evening, the younger men walked ahead, talking with the women who had come to gather firewood in the forest. A neighbour cut an acacia branch and handed it to the father. The father plodded like a three-legged thing. The tiger grass moved sideways in the wind, first lightly, then violently across the mountainside.

At the top of the climb the younger men stopped on a flat boulder. The women sat further along the path. They watched the older men coming up. The father walked second from the last. Below them the Samsu River sighed. It had a conch-shell ring to it.

"No," a man said.

The others sucked their teeth.

They watched the father take two steps, then pause before the next two. He took off his hat and wiped the dampness from his hairless crown. The hat's round brim had collapsed; he fanned his face with it. It turned in his hand — palm-down, palm-up, palm-down — no, no, no.

The last four men reached the top and sat beside the others. Nobody spoke.

A young man stood up, picked up a large stone, and set it on the flat ground beside the path. Others followed with smaller stones, stacking them slowly into a cairn. As the men resumed their walk, each placed something on the stones — a pebble, a twig, a leaf.

The father got up, pressing his stick against a root. He pulled a few fronds of bracken growing under a coral tree. He placed them on the cairn and set a small stone on top. He brought his palms together.
